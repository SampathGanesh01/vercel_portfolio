Most PM frameworks were built for software products. Roadmaps, sprints, user stories, A/B tests — these tools assume you can ship fast, roll back easily, and observe user behaviour in a dashboard.

Robotics breaks all of those assumptions.

## The difference

When I was leading the ORo robot product at Ogmen Robotics, a "bug" wasn't a broken button — it was a robot getting stuck in a corner at 2am, unable to find its way back to the dock. A "feature" wasn't a new settings screen — it was a navigation behaviour that had to work reliably across dozens of different home layouts, floor types, and lighting conditions.

The feedback loop is fundamentally different. You can't watch a session recording to understand why the user churned. You need to read ROS logs, inspect sensor data, and physically walk through the environment the robot was operating in.

## Owning the whole stack

In software PM, you're usually working across frontend and backend. In robotics, the "stack" includes:

- Embedded firmware running on microcontrollers
- A real-time control loop managing motors and sensors
- A navigation stack planning paths through physical space
- Computer vision models interpreting the world
- A cloud backend syncing state with mobile apps
- The physical hardware itself — weight, battery life, motor torque

Every one of these layers has to work together. A change in the navigation planner can affect battery consumption. A firmware update can break the ROS2 communication interface. Your job as PM is to hold all of this in your head while prioritising what actually matters to the user.

## What actually transfers

The fundamentals still apply. You still need to deeply understand your user — what they actually want vs. what they ask for, what friction kills adoption, what moments of delight make them tell others. You still need to ruthlessly prioritise. A robotics team working on 15 things simultaneously will ship nothing that works.

The biggest skill I found transferable was **stakeholder alignment in ambiguity**. When you're building something genuinely new — a robot that autonomously cares for a pet at home — there's no existing benchmark. You're constantly making calls with incomplete data, and your job is to make sure the whole team is aligned on which direction you're betting on.

## The parts nobody tells you about

Hardware has lead times. If you scope a new sensor into a sprint without checking procurement, you'll have engineers blocked for 6 weeks waiting for parts.

Physical testing is slow. You can't "run tests" in CI on a physical robot. Someone has to set up the environment, run the scenario, log the result, and reset. Multiply that across 30 edge cases.

Safety is a first-class feature. In software, you can ship a bug and patch it. In robotics, some bugs are dangerous. You have to build safety thinking into the product process from day one — not as an afterthought.

---

If you're a software PM considering a move into robotics or IoT: it's hard, it's slow, and it's genuinely one of the most interesting product spaces to work in. The constraint of the physical world makes you a better product thinker.
